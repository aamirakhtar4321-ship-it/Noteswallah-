1. Introduction to Real Numbers

Real numbers are the set of all rational and irrational numbers. Every point on the number line represents a unique real number, and every real number corresponds to a unique point on the number line. This chapter builds on your understanding of number systems from earlier classes and introduces powerful tools like Euclid's Division Lemma and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.

Euclid's Division Lemma

For any two positive integers a and b, there exist unique whole numbers q and r such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b. This is the mathematical way of expressing the long division process you've used since primary school — 'a' is the dividend, 'b' is the divisor, 'q' is the quotient, and 'r' is the remainder.

Example: Find the HCF of 135 and 225 using Euclid's algorithm.
225 = 135 × 1 + 90
135 = 90 × 1 + 45
90 = 45 × 2 + 0
Since remainder is 0, HCF = 45.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur. This is why prime numbers are called the "building blocks" of all numbers.

Example: 140 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 7 = 2² × 5 × 7. No matter how you factor 140, you always end up with the same set of prime factors.

Revisiting Irrational Numbers

A number is irrational if it cannot be written in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0. Numbers like √2, √3, √5, and π are irrational. You can prove a number like √2 is irrational using a method called "proof by contradiction" — assuming it IS rational, and showing that this assumption leads to a logical contradiction.
