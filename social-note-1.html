Nationalism in India

History · Social Science

🔖

1. The First World War and Indian Nationalism

The First World War (1914-1918) created a new economic and political situation in India. It led to a huge rise in defence expenditure, financed by war loans and increased taxes. Prices of goods doubled between 1913 and 1918, causing extreme hardship for the common people, while forced recruitment in rural areas caused widespread anger against the British.

The Idea of Satyagraha

Mahatma Gandhi returned to India in 1915 from South Africa, bringing with him a novel method of mass agitation called Satyagraha. The idea emphasised the power of truth and the need to search for truth, suggesting that if the cause was true and the struggle was against injustice, physical force was not necessary to fight the oppressor — one could triumph through non-violent resistance, or Satyagraha.

Key movements led by Gandhi before 1919:
1. Champaran (1917) — fighting against the oppressive plantation system for indigo farmers
2. Kheda (1917) — organised a Satyagraha to support peasants hit by crop failure and plague
3. Ahmedabad (1918) — organised a Satyagraha movement among cotton mill workers

The Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh

In 1919, Gandhi launched a nationwide Satyagraha against the proposed Rowlatt Act (1919), which gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities and allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years. Peaceful protests turned violent in many places. On 13 April 1919, the tragic Jallianwala Bagh massacre occurred in Amritsar, where General Dyer's troops fired on an unarmed crowd gathered for a fair, killing hundreds — an event that shocked the entire nation and turned many moderates towards more radical nationalism.

Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22)

Gandhi proposed that the Non-Cooperation movement should unfold in stages, beginning with the surrender of government-awarded titles, boycott of civil services, army, police, courts, legislative councils, schools, and foreign goods, followed eventually by full civil disobedience if the government continued with repressive measures. The movement gained huge support across India, uniting different social groups — but it was called off in February 1922 after violence broke out in Chauri Chaura, where a police station was set on fire.

The Idea of "India"

As the nationalist movement developed, different groups sought to identify India as a unified nation with a shared history and culture, symbolised through the image of Bharat Mata, folklore, icons, and symbols like the tricolour flag. However, defining "the nation" also created tensions, since not everyone felt the same way about which values or images should represent it, or the same enthusiasm for the movement.
