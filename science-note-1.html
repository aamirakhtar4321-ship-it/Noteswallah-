Chemical Reactions and Equations

Science · Chapter 1

🔖

1. What is a Chemical Reaction?

A chemical reaction is a process in which one or more substances (reactants) are converted into one or more new substances (products) with different chemical properties. You can usually tell a chemical reaction has happened by observing changes like colour change, gas evolution, precipitate formation, change in temperature, or change in state.

Chemical Equations

A chemical equation is the shorthand way of representing a chemical reaction using symbols and formulas instead of long sentences. For example, the reaction between magnesium and oxygen is written as:

Word equation: Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide
Chemical equation: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Balancing a Chemical Equation

A chemical equation must be balanced — the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both the reactant and product side, following the Law of Conservation of Mass. This is done by adjusting coefficients (the numbers placed before formulas), never by changing the subscript numbers inside a formula.

Example: Balance Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
Balanced form: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂

Types of Chemical Reactions

There are five main types you need to know for this chapter:

1. Combination reaction — two or more substances combine to form a single product. Example: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂

2. Decomposition reaction — a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances, often needing heat, light, or electricity. Example: 2FeSO₄ → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃ (on heating)

3. Displacement reaction — a more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound. Example: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu

4. Double displacement reaction — two compounds exchange ions to form two new compounds, often producing a precipitate. Example: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NaCl

5. Oxidation and reduction (redox) — oxidation is the gain of oxygen/loss of hydrogen; reduction is the loss of oxygen/gain of hydrogen. Both always happen together in a redox reaction.

Effects of Oxidation in Everyday Life

Two important real-world effects of oxidation are corrosion (like rusting of iron, where iron reacts with oxygen and moisture to form iron oxide) and rancidity (when fats and oils in food get oxidised and develop a bad smell/taste). Both can be slowed down by adding antioxidants, storing food in airtight containers, or refrigeration.

QUICK RECAP

  • Chemical equations must always be balanced (Law of Conservation of Mass)
  • 5 reaction types: combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, redox
  • Oxidation = gain of oxygen / loss of hydrogen; Reduction = opposite
  • Rusting and rancidity are both real-life oxidation effects
